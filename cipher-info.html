Caesar Cipher

How It Works

The Caesar cipher is a substitution cipher where each letter is shifted a fixed number of positions down the alphabet. With a shift of +3, A becomes D, B becomes E, ..., Z wraps around to C.

Example (shift +3)
Plain: THE QUICK BROWN FOX
Cipher: WKH TXLFN EURZQ IRA

Decryption

Shift backwards by the same amount. If you know the shift, decryption is trivial. If not, try all 25 possibilities (brute force) or use frequency analysis.

Fun Fact

Julius Caesar used this cipher with a shift of +3 for military communications. It wasn't "secure" by modern standards—most of his enemies were illiterate—but it prevented casual snooping.

Sources

  • Suetonius, Life of Julius Caesar 56 – "He wrote to Cicero... substituting the order of the letters of the alphabet."
  • Singh, Simon. The Code Book. Anchor Books, 1999. Ch. 1.
  • Kahn, David. The Codebreakers. Scribner, 1996.

ROT13

How It Works

ROT13 ("rotate by 13") is a special case of the Caesar cipher with a fixed shift of 13. Since the alphabet has 26 letters, applying ROT13 twice returns the original text—encryption and decryption are identical operations.

Example
Plain: WHY DID THE CHICKEN CROSS THE ROAD
Cipher: JUL QVG GUR PVPXRAP PEBFF GUR EBNQ

Properties

Self-inverse: ROT13(ROT13(text)) = text. No key needed—always shift 13. Not secure; trivially reversible.

Fun Fact

ROT13 became popular on Usenet in the 1980s to hide spoilers, punchlines, and offensive content—not for secrecy, but so readers had to deliberately choose to decode it. It's still used in geocaching hints and some programming puzzles today.

Sources

  • RFC 1321 – "The ROT13 Algorithm" (informal, April 1992).
  • Raymond, Eric S. The New Hacker's Dictionary. MIT Press, 1996.
  • Usenet archives: rec.humor, alt.folklore.computers.

Atbash Cipher

How It Works

Atbash reverses the alphabet: A↔Z, B↔Y, C↔X, ..., M↔N. It's a fixed substitution with no key—like ROT13, encryption and decryption are identical.

Example
Plain: ATBASH CIPHER
Cipher: ZGYZHS XRKSVI

History

One of the earliest known ciphers, used in the Hebrew Bible. The name comes from the first, last, second, and second-to-last letters of the Hebrew alphabet: Aleph (א) ↔ Tav (ת), Bet (ב) ↔ Shin (ש).

Fun Fact

Atbash appears in the Book of Jeremiah (25:26, 51:41) where "Sheshach" (ששך) decodes to "Babylon" (בבל) and "Leb Kamai" decodes to "Chaldeans." It's one of the few ciphers explicitly documented in ancient religious texts.

Sources

  • Jeremiah 25:26, 51:41 (Hebrew Bible).
  • Gematria/Atbash in Jewish hermeneutics – Encyclopaedia Judaica.
  • Kahn, David. The Codebreakers. Scribner, 1996. Ch. 2.

A1Z26 Cipher

How It Works

Each letter is replaced by its position in the alphabet: A=1, B=2, C=3, ..., Z=26. Numbers are typically space-separated. Not a substitution cipher in the traditional sense—it's an encoding.

Example
Plain: HELLO WORLD
Cipher: 8 5 12 12 15 23 15 18 12 4

Properties

No key, trivially reversible. Preserves word boundaries (spaces). Vulnerable to frequency analysis on numbers. Often combined with other ciphers for puzzles.

Fun Fact

A1Z26 is the basis for many "number station" broadcasts during the Cold War—spies would receive number sequences decoded via one-time pads. It's also used in geocaching, puzzle hunts, and the "Captain Midnight" decoder badges from 1940s radio shows.

Sources

  • Barker, Wayne G. Cryptanalysis of Number Systems. Aegean Park Press, 1982.
  • Kahn, David. The Codebreakers. Scribner, 1996. (Discussion of figure ciphers).
  • Various puzzle hunt archives (MIT Mystery Hunt, Puzzled Pint).

Binary Cipher

How It Works

Each letter is converted to its 5-bit binary representation based on its position in the alphabet: A=1 (00001), B=2 (00010), ..., Z=26 (11010). Binary groups are typically space-separated.

Example
Plain: HELLO WORLD
Cipher: 01000 00101 01100 01100 01111 10111 01111 10010 01100 00100

Properties

Fixed-width encoding (5 bits per letter). No key required. Preserves word boundaries. Can be transmitted as on/off signals (Morse, digital). Vulnerable to frequency analysis.

Fun Fact

5-bit binary encoding (Baudot code) was used in early telegraph systems and teleprinters. The 5-bit limit allows 32 codes (26 letters + space + punctuation + control codes). Modern ASCII uses 7 or 8 bits for more characters.

Sources

  • Baudot, Émile. "Telegraphic Apparatus." French Patent 171,675 (1874).
  • Garner, Robert. "The Baudot Code and Early Telegraphy." IEEE Annals of the History of Computing 12.1 (1990): 32–41.
  • Kahn, David. The Codebreakers. Scribner, 1996. Ch. 5 (Teleprinter ciphers).

Morse Code

How It Works

Morse code encodes letters as sequences of dots (.) and dashes (-). The most frequent letters get the shortest codes: E = ., T = -. Letters are separated by spaces, words by / or double spaces.

Example
Plain: HELLO WORLD
Cipher: .... . .-.. .-.. --- / .-- --- .-. .-.. -..

Properties

Variable-length encoding optimized for letter frequency. No key required. Can be transmitted as sound, light, or electrical pulses. Originally designed for telegraph lines.

Fun Fact

The SOS distress signal (... --- ...) was chosen because it's unmistakable and easy to transmit, not because it stands for "Save Our Souls." It was adopted as the international distress signal in 1908.

Sources

  • Morse, Samuel F. B. "Improvement in the Mode of Communicating Information by Signals." U.S. Patent 1,647 (1840).
  • Hurowitz, Richard. The Telegraph: A History of Morse's Invention and Its Predecessors in the United States. McFarland, 2012.
  • International Telecommunication Union. "Morse Code." ITU-R M.1677-1 (2009).
